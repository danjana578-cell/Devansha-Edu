8.1 Rediscovering the Roots of Atomic Theory

From ancient Indian & Greek philosophy to John Dalton's scientific postulate

Everything in the universe, from non-living objects (a brick wall, a house) to living organisms (cells, tissues, humans), is composed of matter. For centuries, human curiosity asked: Is matter continuous, or is it made of tiny indivisible particles?

Ancient Philosophical Origins

  • Acharya Kanada (Ancient India, ~600 BCE): In his Sanskrit philosophical text Vaisesika Sutras, he proposed that if matter (dravya) is divided repeatedly, you reach an indivisible ultimate unit called Parmanu (परमाणु). He described combinations of parmanus forming dyads (pairs of two) and triads (groups of three) that constitute all substances.
  • Democritus and Leucippus (Ancient Greece, ~400 BCE): Proposed that dividing matter leads to unbreakable particles termed Atomos (in Greek, atomos = indivisible/uncuttable).
📖 John Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

British schoolteacher John Dalton provided the first scientific foundation for atoms based on the chemical laws of conservation of mass and definite proportions. He proposed that:

  1. All matter is made of indivisible particles called atoms.
  2. Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.
  3. Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  4. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.

8.2 A Short Historical Journey Through Atomic Models

How discovery of subatomic particles shattered Dalton's indivisible atom

In the late 19th century, the discovery of Radioactivity and Cathode Rays proved that atoms are NOT indivisible, but composed of smaller subatomic particles (electrons, protons, neutrons).

J. J. Thomson (1856–1940) — Discovery of the Electron (1897)

Studying electric discharge in high-voltage vacuum tubes (Cathode Ray Tube), Thomson observed rays traveling from the cathode (−) to anode (+). These were streams of light, negatively charged particles named Electrons ($e^-$) with a charge of $-1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$ and negligible mass ($\approx \frac{1}{1840}$ of hydrogen atom). Thomson received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.

8.2.1 Thomson's "Plum Pudding" or Watermelon Model

Since atoms are electrically neutral overall, Thomson proposed in 1904:

  • An atom is a solid sphere of uniform positive charge.
  • Negatively charged electrons are embedded inside it, like plums in a pudding or black seeds inside the red pulp of a watermelon.
  • Success: Successfully explained electrical neutrality (Total positive charge = Total negative charge).
  • Limitation: Failed to explain how positive and negative charges were held together without neutralizing each other, and could not explain scattering experiments.

8.2.2 Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment ($\alpha$-Ray Scattering)

The experiment that discovered the atomic nucleus (1911)

In 1911, Ernest Rutherford (assisted by Geiger and Marsden) bombarded a beam of fast-moving, positively charged Alpha particles ($\alpha$-particles, $^4_2\text{He}^{2+}$) on an ultra-thin gold sheet (~1000 atoms thick, $100\text{ nm}$).

Observation ObservedConclusion / Deduction
1. Most $\alpha$-particles (~99.9%) passed straight through without any deflection. Most of the space inside an atom is empty.
2. A small fraction was deflected by small and large angles. Positive charge is not spread out, but concentrated in a very tiny region that repelled the positive $\alpha$-particles.
3. Very few (1 in ~20,000) bounced back completely ($180^\circ$ deflection). The Nucleus is extremely dense, rigid, and heavy containing almost all the mass of the atom in an infinitesimal volume.
Scale Analogy (Cricket Ground vs Black Pepper)
The nucleus is about $10^5$ (1 Lakh) times smaller than the whole atom! If an atom were as large as a full Cricket Stadium (~100 m wide), the nucleus at the center would be the size of a tiny grain of black pepper (~1 mm)!

Rutherford's Planetary Model

+

Electrons orbit the tiny central positive nucleus like planets orbiting the Sun.

Limitations of Rutherford's Model (Why Atoms Didn't Collapse)

According to classical electromagnetic theory (Maxwell), any charged particle (electron) moving in a circular path undergoes continuous acceleration, radiating energy continuously. As it loses energy, its orbit must rapidly spiral inward, crashing into the nucleus within $10^{-8}$ seconds! But in reality, matter is stable and atoms never spontaneously collapse.

8.2.3 Bohr's Model of the Atom (1913)

Postulates of discrete energy levels and stationary orbits

Niels Bohr (1885–1962) — Quantum Stationary Orbits

To overcome Rutherford's instability dilemma, Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed his pathbreaking quantum postulates in 1913, earning the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922.

Key Postulates of Bohr's Model

  1. Discrete Stationary Orbits: Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain allowed, non-radiating circular paths called stationary states, orbits, or energy shells.
  2. No Energy Loss in Fixed Orbit: While revolving in a permissible shell, an electron does NOT radiate energy.
  3. Energy Levels ($K, L, M, N...$): Shells are designated by integers $n = 1, 2, 3, 4...$ or letters $K, L, M, N...$. Energy increases outward: $E_K < E_L < E_M < E_N$.
  4. Quantum Energy Jumps: An electron absorbs energy to jump to a higher shell (excited state) and emits a photon of energy ($\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h\nu$) when falling back to a lower shell.
Curiosity: Why K, L, M, N instead of A, B, C, D?
Charles Barkla (pioneer of X-ray spectroscopy) labeled the first observed X-ray absorption lines starting from 'K' to leave alphabetic room (A through J) in case higher-energy lines were discovered later. Bohr adopted this established notation!

8.3 Discovery of the Neutron & Subatomic Particle Summary

James Chadwick (1932) and the Strong Nuclear Force

Helium has 2 protons, yet its atomic mass is 4 times that of Hydrogen (1 proton). In 1932, James Chadwick bombarded Beryllium with $\alpha$-particles and discovered uncharged particles with mass nearly equal to a proton — named Neutrons ($n^0$).

🧲 Why don't positively charged protons push each other out of the nucleus?

Protons repel each other electrostatically. Neutrons act as a nuclear buffer and provide additional attractive Strong Nuclear Force (the strongest fundamental force in nature) that overcomes electrostatic repulsion and binds nucleons together tightly.

8.4 – 8.6 Chemical Symbols, Atomic Number ($Z$) & Mass Number ($A$)

Standard representation: $^A_Z\text{X}$

Berzelius IUPAC Symbol Rules

  • First letter is always capitalized: Carbon $\to$ C, Nitrogen $\to$ N.
  • Second letter is always lowercase: Aluminium $\to$ Al (never AL), Cobalt $\to$ Co (CO is Carbon Monoxide!).

8.7 Electronic Configuration (Bohr-Bury Scheme)

Rules for electron distribution across shells ($K, L, M, N$)

Bohr-Bury Rules:

  1. $2n^2$ Rule: Maximum capacity of $n^{\text{th}}$ shell = $2n^2$.
    • $K\text{-shell } (n=1) = 2(1)^2 = \mathbf{2}$
    • $L\text{-shell } (n=2) = 2(2)^2 = \mathbf{8}$
    • $M\text{-shell } (n=3) = 2(3)^2 = \mathbf{18}$
    • $N\text{-shell } (n=4) = 2(4)^2 = \mathbf{32}$
  2. Octet Rule: The outermost valence shell can never accommodate more than 8 electrons.
  3. Stepwise Filling: Inner shells must be completely filled before electrons occupy outer shells.

8.8 Combining Capacity: Valency

Why atoms react to achieve noble gas electron stability
📖 Definition: Valency

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom — the number of electrons an atom loses, gains, or shares to achieve an octet (8 valence electrons) or duplet (2 electrons in K shell).

8.9 Isotopes and Isobars

Twin atoms and identical weight atoms

Isotopes

Atoms of the same element with same atomic number ($Z$) but different mass numbers ($A$) due to differing neutron counts.

  • Hydrogen: Protium ($^1_1\text{H}$), Deuterium ($^2_1\text{H}$), Tritium ($^3_1\text{H}$).
  • Carbon: $^{12}_6\text{C}, ^{13}_6\text{C}, ^{14}_6\text{C}$.
  • Chlorine: $^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$ (75%) and $^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$ (25%).
  • Chemical properties are identical; physical properties differ.

Isobars

Atoms of different elements having different atomic numbers ($Z$) but the same mass number ($A$).

  • $^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ (Argon: $p=18, n=22$)
  • $^{40}_{19}\text{K}$ (Potassium: $p=19, n=21$)
  • $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$ (Calcium: $p=20, n=20$)
  • All have mass number 40 but completely different chemical properties!

Average Atomic Mass of Chlorine (Weighted Average Formula)

In nature, $^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}\text{Cl}$ exist in a $3:1$ ratio (75% and 25%):

\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \left(35 \times \frac{75}{100}\right) + \left(37 \times \frac{25}{100}\right) = \frac{105}{4} + \frac{37}{4} = \frac{142}{4} = \mathbf{35.5\text{ u}}

Crucial Real-Life Applications of Radioisotopes

  • Uranium-235 ($^{235}_{92}\text{U}$): Nuclear reactor fuel to generate clean electricity.
  • Cobalt-60 ($^{60}_{27}\text{Co}$): Radiotherapy treatment of malignant cancer tumors.
  • Iodine-131 ($^{131}_{53}\text{I}$): Medical diagnosis and treatment of Goitre / Thyroid disorders.
  • Carbon-14 ($^{14}_6\text{C}$): Radiocarbon dating to determine the exact age of ancient fossils and archaeological artifacts.

India's Nuclear Pioneer: Dr. Homi J. Bhabha & BARC (Dhruva Reactor)

Dr. Homi Jehangir Bhabha founded TIFR and BARC in Mumbai. India's high-flux research reactor Dhruva produces critical radioisotopes for cancer treatment, agriculture seed mutation, and advanced neutron-scattering material research.

Q2. Which statements are correct according to Bohr's Model?

• (i) Incorrect: Electrons in stationary orbits do not lose energy.
• (ii) & (iv) Incorrect: Electrons are strictly quantized in allowed shells ($n=1,2,3...$) and cannot exist in intermediate non-quantized spaces.
Q3. Atomic species relation: X ($p=18, n=19$), Y ($p=17, n=18$), Z ($p=17, n=20$)
• (i) Relation between Y and Z: Both have $p = 17$ (same element, Chlorine) but different mass numbers ($A_Y = 17+18 = 35$, $A_Z = 17+20 = 37$). Hence, Y and Z are ISOTOPES.
• (ii) Relation between Z and X: $A_Z = 17+20 = 37$; $A_X = 18+19 = 37$. Both have the same mass number ($A=37$) but different atomic numbers (17 vs 18). Hence, Z and X are ISOBARS.
Q4. Riddle: "I have mass number 23 and 11 protons. I am a soft metal and react vigorously with water. Who am I?"
• Identity: Sodium (Na) ($Z=11$).
• Number of Neutrons: $n = A - Z = 23 - 11 = \mathbf{12\text{ neutrons}}$.
Q5. Element X has mass number 35 and 18 neutrons. Complete numerical analysis
(i) Protons = $A - n = 35 - 18 = \mathbf{17}$; Electrons = $\mathbf{17}$.
(ii) Atomic number $Z = \mathbf{17}$.
(iii) Element X is Chlorine (Cl).
(iv) Electronic configuration: $\mathbf{2, 8, 7}$ ($K=2, L=8, M=7$).
(v) Valence electrons = $\mathbf{7}$; Valency = $8 - 7 = \mathbf{1}$.
(vi) If 2 neutrons are added: New mass number $A' = 35 + 2 = \mathbf{37}$.
(vii) Relation: The new atom ($^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$) is an ISOTOPE of X ($^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$).
