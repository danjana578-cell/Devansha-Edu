Q 01
What is a cell? Why is it called the basic structural and functional unit of life?
A cell is the smallest, membrane-bound unit capable of carrying out all life processes independently.
• Structural Unit: Every living organism is physically constructed of one or more cells (like bricks in a building).
• Functional Unit: All vital metabolic functions (respiration, energy generation, protein synthesis, excretion) occur inside cells.

Q 03
Explain Cell Theory. Mention the contributions of Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann and Rudolf Virchow.
Cell Theory is the foundational principle of cell biology:
1. Matthias Schleiden (1838): Concluded that all plant tissues are composed of cells.
2. Theodor Schwann (1839): Concluded that all animal tissues are made of cells and postulated that cells are the basic unit of life.
3. Rudolf Virchow (1855): Added the third tenet — "Omnis cellula e cellula" (All living cells arise only from pre-existing cells through division).

Q 06 & 07
Explain the structure & functions of the cell membrane. Why is it called selectively permeable?
• Structure: Described by Singer & Nicolson's Fluid Mosaic Model as a flexible phospholipid bilayer with embedded intrinsic and peripheral proteins.
• Selectively Permeable: It regulates entry and exit by allowing only certain solvent and solute molecules (like O₂, CO₂, glucose, water) to pass through while restricting harmful or unwanted molecules.
• Functions: Provides boundary protection, cellular recognition, signal transduction, and active/passive transport.
Q 08 & 09
Differentiate between diffusion and osmosis. Explain isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic solutions.
• Diffusion: Spontaneous movement of any substance (gas/liquid/solute) from high concentration to low concentration without requiring a semi-permeable membrane.
• Osmosis: Movement of water molecules from high water concentration (dilute) to low water concentration (concentrated) across a selectively permeable membrane.

Solution Types & Cell Effects:
1. Hypotonic (Dilute outside): Water enters the cell (Endosmosis) → Animal cell swells and may burst (Lysis); Plant cell becomes firm & Turgid.
2. Isotonic (Equal concentration): Net movement is zero → Cell remains normal/flaccid.
3. Hypertonic (Concentrated outside): Water leaves the cell (Exosmosis) → Animal cell shrinks (Crenation); Plant cell cytoplasm pulls away from cell wall (Plasmolysis).
Q 10
Explain the structure and functions of the cell wall.
• Structure: A rigid, non-living, permeable layer outside the plasma membrane composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin.
• Functions: (1) Gives definite shape and mechanical strength to plant cells. (2) Exerts inward turgor pressure preventing cells from bursting in hypotonic media. (3) Connects adjacent cells via cytoplasmic bridges called Plasmodesmata.
Q 11 & 12
Explain the structure of the nucleus. Differentiate between DNA, gene, chromatin and chromosome.
• Structure of Nucleus: Enclosed by a double-layered nuclear envelope with nuclear pores, containing a gel-like nucleoplasm, a dense nucleolus (rRNA & ribosome synthesis site), and genetic material.

Genetic Hierarchy:
• DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid): The double-helix master chemical molecule carrying all hereditary instructions.
• Gene: A functional segment/unit of DNA that codes for a specific protein or phenotypic trait.
• Chromatin: The loose, uncoiled, thread-like complex of DNA and histone proteins visible during non-dividing (interphase) state.
• Chromosome: The highly condensed, rod-like structures of chromatin formed during cell division (Humans have 46 chromosomes / 23 pairs).
Q 13
What are ribosomes? Why are they called the protein factories of the cell?
Ribosomes are non-membrane-bound dense ribonucleoprotein particles made of rRNA and ribosomal proteins. They translate genetic code (mRNA) into polypeptide chains (proteins). Because all structural, enzymatic, and regulatory proteins are synthesized on them, they are nicknamed the "Protein Factories of the Cell".
Q 14
Explain Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER). Differentiate between Rough ER (RER) and Smooth ER (SER).
The ER is an extensive intracellular network of interconnected membranous tubules, sheets, and cisternae.
• Rough ER (RER): Surface is studded with ribosomes; primary site of protein synthesis and transport.
• Smooth ER (SER): Lacks ribosomes; synthesizes lipids, phospholipids, and steroid hormones; detoxifies drugs and poisons in liver cells.
Q 15
Explain Golgi Apparatus. Why is it called the packaging and shipping centre of the cell?
Discovered by Camillo Golgi, it consists of stacks of membrane-bound, parallel flattened sacs called cisternae.
It receives synthesized proteins and lipids from the ER at its cis face, chemically modifies them (glycosylation), packs them into secretory vesicles, and dispatches them to targets (plasma membrane, lysosomes, extracellular space). Hence, it is the cell's "Packaging & Dispatch Center".
Q 16
What are lysosomes? Why are they called the clean-up system / suicidal bags of the cell?
Lysosomes are single-membrane spherical vesicles containing powerful hydrolytic digestive enzymes (hydrolases) active in acidic pH.
• Clean-up System: They engulf and digest foreign bacteria, cellular debris, and worn-out organelles (autophagy).
• Suicide Bags: In damaged or dying cells, lysosomes can rupture and release their digestive enzymes, digesting their own parent cell completely (autolysis / apoptosis).
Q 17
Why are mitochondria called the powerhouse of the cell? Explain their structure.
• Powerhouse: Mitochondria carry out cellular aerobic respiration, breaking down pyruvic acid to generate ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) — the universal energy currency.
• Structure: Double-membraned. Outer membrane is smooth; inner membrane is deeply folded into finger-like projections called cristae (providing large surface area for ATP-generating oxysomes / ATP synthase). Inside is the protein-rich matrix containing its own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes (semi-autonomous).
Q 18 & 19
Explain Chloroplast & Plastids. Differentiate between Chloroplasts, Chromoplasts and Leucoplasts.
Plastids are double-membrane organelles found exclusively in plant cells and algae:
1. Chloroplasts: Contain green pigment chlorophyll and carotenoids; site of photosynthesis (kitchen of the cell). Structure contains thylakoid stacks (grana) and fluid stroma with own DNA/ribosomes.
2. Chromoplasts: Contain colored pigments (carotenes, xanthophylls); give vibrant yellow, orange, and red colors to flowers and ripening fruits to attract pollinators.
3. Leucoplasts: Colorless storage plastids — Amyloplasts (store starch/potatoes), Elaioplasts (store oils/lipids), Aleuroplasts (store proteins).
Q 20
What are vacuoles? Differentiate between plant vacuoles and animal vacuoles.
Vacuoles are membrane-bound storage organelles enclosed by a single semi-permeable membrane called the tonoplast.
• Plant Vacuoles: Immense central vacuole occupying 50–90% of cell volume, filled with cell sap; provides turgidity, rigidity, and stores metabolic wastes/pigments.
• Animal Vacuoles: Small, transient, and multiple; involved in pinocytosis, phagocytosis (food vacuoles in Amoeba), or osmoregulation (contractile vacuoles).

Q 25 & 26
What is contact inhibition? Differentiate between normal cells and cancer cells.
• Contact Inhibition: A natural regulatory property of normal cells where contact with neighboring cells halts uncontrolled cell division.
• Cancer Cells: Cancer cells lose contact inhibition, dividing uncontrollably to form masses called tumors.
• Normal vs Cancer: Normal cells have a finite lifespan (Hayflick limit), regular shape, and regulated division. Cancer cells are immortal, have irregular nuclei, abnormal chromosome numbers, and can invade distant tissues (metastasis).
Q 27
What is totipotency? Mention the contribution of Gottlieb Haberlandt.
Totipotency is the inherent biological capacity of a single plant cell to divide and regenerate into an entire whole functional organism. Austrian botanist Gottlieb Haberlandt (1902) first conceived the concept of plant tissue culture and is recognized as the Father of Plant Tissue Culture.
Q 28
What is Programmed Cell Death (PCD / Apoptosis)? Explain its importance with an example.
Programmed Cell Death (Apoptosis) is a genetically controlled, orderly biochemical process of cellular self-destruction.
• Importance & Example: During human embryonic development, fingers and toes are initially joined by webbed tissue; apoptosis eliminates the interdigital cells to separate individual fingers. In tadpole metamorphosis, the entire tail is resorbed via apoptosis to form an adult frog!
Q 29
Explain the onion root tip experiment used to study cell division.
The apical meristem of onion root tips contains actively dividing cells. Roots are grown over water, excised, fixed in acetic alcohol, stained with acetocarmine, and squashed under a cover glass. Under a microscope, distinct mitotic stages (Prophase, Metaphase with equatorial plate alignment, Anaphase with chromatid separation, and Telophase with cell plate formation) are clearly observed.
Q 30–37
Quick Difference Sheet: Important Differentiations for Exam
• Cell Membrane vs Cell Wall: Cell membrane is living, thin, lipid-protein based, selectively permeable. Cell wall is dead, thick, cellulose based, freely permeable.
• Chloroplast vs Mitochondria: Chloroplast carries out anabolic photosynthesis to store energy as glucose (plants only). Mitochondria carries out catabolic respiration to release energy as ATP (plants & animals).
• Benign vs Malignant Tumor: Benign remains localized and harmless. Malignant grows rapidly, invades neighboring tissues, and spreads via blood/lymph (metastasis).
• Virus vs Viroid vs Prion: Virus has nucleic acid (DNA/RNA) + protein coat. Viroid consists ONLY of infectious naked RNA (no protein). Prion consists ONLY of infectious misfolded protein (no nucleic acid).
Q 38
Explain the role of ATP in cells. Why is ATP called the energy currency of the cell?
ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) stores chemical energy in high-energy terminal phosphate bonds. Hydrolysis of ATP into ADP + Pi releases ~30.5 kJ/mol of usable energy that instantly drives cellular processes (muscle contraction, nerve impulse transmission, active transport, protein synthesis). Just as money is exchanged for goods and services in daily life, cells "spend" ATP whenever energy is needed!
Q 39 & 40
Light Microscope Parts & Why onion peel cells are regular while cheek cells are irregular in shape?
• Microscope Parts: Eyepiece/Ocular lens (magnifies image), Objective lenses (4x, 10x, 40x, 100x), Stage & Clips (holds slide), Condenser & Diaphragm (focuses light), Coarse & Fine Adjustment Knobs (sharp focusing).
• Onion vs Cheek Cell Shape: Onion peel cells possess a rigid, structural cellulose cell wall that forces them into a fixed, brick-like rectangular shape. Human cheek (epithelial) cells lack a cell wall and have only a flexible plasma membrane, resulting in an irregular, rounded, pliable boundary.
